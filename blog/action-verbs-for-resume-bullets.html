10 Action Verbs That Make Your Resume Bullet Points Stronger

CareerCanvas Editorial Team · 7 min read

"Responsible for managing the weekly team meeting." Read that sentence again — what did the person actually do? Did the meeting run better because of them, or would it have happened anyway? "Responsible for" describes a duty, not an outcome, and a resume full of duties reads like a job description, not a record of what you accomplished.

The fix isn't complicated: swap a duty-based phrase for a verb that describes an action and, where possible, a result. This isn't about sounding impressive for its own sake — it's about giving a hiring manager (and an applicant tracking system scanning for relevant terms) something concrete to evaluate.

Ten verbs, and when to use them

Different verbs suit different kinds of work. Here are ten reliable options grouped by what they're good at describing:

When you started or built something

  • Launched — for a product, campaign, feature, or initiative that didn't exist before you started it.
  • Built — for something you constructed from scratch, whether that's a spreadsheet system, a codebase, or a client relationship.
  • Designed — for the planning and structure behind a process, system, or piece of content, even if someone else executed part of it.

When you made something better

  • Improved — for incremental gains to an existing process, product, or metric.
  • Streamlined — specifically for cutting steps, time, or friction out of a workflow.
  • Reduced — pairs well with a number: costs, errors, turnaround time, churn.

When you led people or delivered results

  • Led — for managing a team, a project, or a cross-functional effort, even informally.
  • Coordinated — for aligning multiple people or teams without necessarily managing them directly.
  • Delivered — for completing a defined piece of work on time or under a constraint.
  • Increased — pairs with a number: revenue, engagement, retention, output.

Before and after examples

Seeing the change in context matters more than the list of verbs on its own. Here are five common bullet points rewritten:

Before: "Responsible for managing social media accounts and posting content."
After: "Managed three social media accounts and built a consistent content calendar, increasing monthly engagement by 22%."
Before: "In charge of customer support tickets."
After: "Resolved an average of 40 customer support tickets weekly, maintaining a 95% satisfaction rating."
Before: "Helped organize the company's annual conference."
After: "Coordinated logistics for a 200-person annual conference, managing vendor contracts and an $18,000 event budget."
Before: "Worked on improving the onboarding process for new hires."
After: "Streamlined the new-hire onboarding process, reducing average ramp-up time from three weeks to ten days."
Before: "Duties included data entry and report generation."
After: "Built a weekly reporting template that cut manual data-entry time by roughly four hours per week."

Notice that most of the "after" versions also add a number. You don't need an exact, audited figure — a reasonable estimate ("roughly," "an average of," "about") is honest and still far more useful to a reader than no number at all.

What if you genuinely don't have a number?

Not every task produces an easy metric, and that's fine. In those cases, focus on scope and specificity instead:

  • Instead of a percentage, describe the scale: "supported a team of 12," "managed inventory across 3 store locations."
  • Instead of an outcome metric, describe the deliverable: "produced a 40-page onboarding guide now used across the department."
  • If you're a student or fresher without workplace metrics, use project or coursework results: "built a mobile app used by over 50 classmates during a semester-long project."

A word of caution

Strong verbs should describe what actually happened, not stretch it. If you coordinated a meeting, "coordinated" is accurate — "spearheaded" might not be. Employers do ask follow-up questions about resume claims in interviews, and a bullet point that oversells your role is easy to notice once someone asks a specific question about it. The goal is precision, not exaggeration.
