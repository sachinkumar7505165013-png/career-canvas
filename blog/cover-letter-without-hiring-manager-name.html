How to Write a Cover Letter When You Don't Know the Hiring Manager's Name

CareerCanvas Editorial Team · 5 min read

Plenty of advice tells you to always address a cover letter to a specific person, and it's good advice when it's possible — a named greeting reads as more personal and shows you did a bit of homework. But a lot of job postings, especially from larger companies or those going through a recruiting platform, simply don't tell you who's reading it. In that case, chasing down a name can eat up time you don't have, and guessing wrong ("Dear Mr. Smith" when the hiring manager is a woman named Alex) is worse than not naming anyone at all.

Here's a practical approach: try a little, then use a solid fallback.

Quick ways to try to find a name (five minutes, not fifty)

  • Check the job posting itself for any named contact, even in the fine print or application instructions.
  • Look at the company's team or leadership page for a "Head of [Department]" or "Talent Acquisition" title that matches the role.
  • Search the company name plus the role's department on a professional networking site to see if a recruiter or hiring manager for that team is visible.
  • If you have any mutual connection to the company, a quick message asking who's hiring for the role can save you the guesswork entirely.

If none of that turns up a confident answer within a few minutes, stop looking and use one of the fallbacks below — spending an hour hunting for a name is rarely worth the trade-off against actually tailoring the letter's content.

Better alternatives to "To Whom It May Concern"

"To Whom It May Concern" isn't wrong, but it reads as dated and impersonal. A few options that sound more current:

  • "Dear Hiring Manager," — simple, standard, and safe. This is the most common modern default and reads perfectly professionally.
  • "Dear [Team Name] Team," — for example, "Dear Product Team," or "Dear Customer Success Team," if the posting makes the department clear. This can feel a bit warmer without guessing at an individual's name.
  • "Dear [Company Name] Recruiting Team," — a reasonable option when you're applying through a general careers page rather than to a specific department.

Whichever you choose, keep it consistent with the tone of the rest of your letter — a very formal greeting followed by a casual, conversational letter (or the reverse) can feel a little disjointed.

The greeting matters less than what comes next

It's worth keeping some perspective here: a generic-but-professional greeting has never been the reason a strong cover letter gets rejected. What actually earns attention is the first two sentences after the greeting. Compare:

"Dear Hiring Manager, I am writing to apply for the Marketing Coordinator position I saw advertised on your website."
"Dear Hiring Manager, Your Marketing Coordinator posting mentions growing organic social reach — that's exactly the work I've spent the last two years doing at [Company], where I grew one platform's following by 60% in six months."

The second version does more work in one sentence than most greetings do in a whole paragraph, because it connects something specific from the job posting to something specific and real about the candidate. If you only have time to fix one part of your cover letter, it's this — not the greeting.

A short structure that works for most letters

  1. Greeting — pick one of the options above.
  2. Opening line — connect a specific detail from the job posting to a specific detail from your background.
  3. One or two supporting sentences — a concrete example (a project, a result, a skill applied in practice) that backs up the opening claim.
  4. A closing line — what you're hoping happens next, stated plainly ("I'd welcome the chance to discuss how I could contribute to your team").
  5. Sign-off — "Best regards" or "Sincerely," followed by your name.

This whole structure fits comfortably in three short paragraphs. A cover letter doesn't need to retell your entire resume — it needs to make the reader want to open it.
